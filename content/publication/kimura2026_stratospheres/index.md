abstract: 'Context. Thermal inversions are usually interpreted as signatures of high-altitude absorption of stellar radiation. In self-luminous atmospheres, such as those of isolated brown dwarfs and young giant planets, external irradiation is negligible, and 1D radiative-equilibrium models are generally expected to produce monotonically decreasing temperature profiles. Recent retrievals of isolated brown dwarfs, however, have suggested upper-atmosphere thermal inversions, challenging the conventional picture that such inversions require external heating. Aims. We investigate whether thermal inversions can arise in self-luminous atmospheres solely through the redistribution of internal flux between wavelength bands. Rather than targeting a specific object, we ask whether radiative mechanisms alone can generate inversion-like structures without external irradiation. Methods. We developed a 1D, plane-parallel two-band radiative-transfer model under the two-stream approximation. The atmosphere is in radiative equilibrium and is powered only by a prescribed internal flux. Using phenomenological opacity profiles, we explored how wavelength-dependent energy transport and deposition can shape the thermal structure. Results. Our simulations reveal that thermal inversions can emerge even when the only energy source is internal heat. Radiation from deep, hot layers is transported upwards in the short-wavelength band, which heats the gas in the inversion region. This heating is balanced by cooling in the long-wavelength band. The two examples produce inversions through enhanced short-wavelength absorption or an outward decline in long-wavelength opacity. Both illustrate how opacity-dependent redistribution of the internal flux can produce a temperature rise with altitude while maintaining local radiative equilibrium, although these represent only two cases among many possible opacity configurations. Conclusions. These results point to a broader possibility: thermal inversions do not necessarily require an external energy source. In a self-luminous atmosphere, such as those of recently observed brown-dwarfs and young planets, wavelength-dependent redistribution of the internal heat flux could itself generate inversion-like structures.'
